India's first step into space

Why was Thumba chosen to launch rockets?

A small fishing village near Thiruvananthapuram, Kerala. A church by the sea. And an invisible line in Earth's magnetic field. This page explains how they came together on 21 November 1963.

Step 1

Earth has two "equators"

Most people know only one. Thumba was picked because of the second one.

🌍 Geographic equator (0° latitude)

  • The middle line of Earth, halfway between the North and South Poles.
  • Fixed. It never moves.
  • Passes through Ecuador, Brazil, Kenya, Indonesia and others. Not through India.

🧲 Magnetic equator (dip equator)

  • Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet.
  • A free magnetic needle tilts ("dips") down toward the ground. This tilt is called the dip angle.
  • On the magnetic equator, the dip is zero. The needle lies flat, because the magnetic field runs parallel to the ground.
  • It is a wavy line, not straight. It also drifts slowly over the years.
  • In India it passes over the southern tip, near Thiruvananthapuram.

💡 Common mix-up: Big satellite launchers like to be near the geographic equator, because Earth's spin gives them a free push. Thumba was not chosen for that. It was chosen to study the air and electric currents high above the magnetic equator. India's satellite launches happen from Sriharikota, a different site.
Step 2

The hidden "river of electricity" in the sky

Above the magnetic equator there is something special to study: the Equatorial Electrojet.

  • About 100–110 km above the ground sits the ionosphere, a layer of charged (electrically active) air made by sunlight.
  • During the day, winds and sunlight drive an electric current there.
  • Right over the magnetic equator, where the field lines are flat, this current becomes very strong. It flows from west to east.
  • It is a narrow ribbon, only about ±3° of latitude wide on either side of the magnetic equator.
  • This affects radio signals, GPS accuracy and satellite communication. Scientists badly wanted to measure it.
  • Balloons cannot go that high. Satellites fly much higher. So sounding rockets (short research rockets that go up and fall back) are the best tool.
🎯 To fly a rocket through the electrojet, you must launch from inside that narrow ±3° band. In India, only the southern tip qualifies.

Step 4

So why Thumba, and not anywhere else?

The magnetic equator crosses the tip of India over a stretch of the Kerala and Tamil Nadu coast. Scientists checked many sites. Here is why Thumba won. Tap each card.

🧲

Close to the magnetic equator

The main rule for picking the site.

The rocket had to fly through the electrojet band. Thumba (8.54° N) sat very close to the magnetic equator in the 1960s, well inside the ±3° band.
🌊

Sea on the west side

Safe place for rockets to fall.

Spent rocket stages drop into the Arabian Sea, away from homes. Nobody on land is in danger.
✈️

Airport next door

Easy to bring in rockets and equipment.

Thiruvananthapuram airport is right beside Thumba. Rockets and instruments from the USA, France and the USSR could be flown in quickly.
🏙️

Near a capital city

Roads, power, workshops and people.

Thiruvananthapuram, the state capital, gave labs, housing, offices and skilled workers. The Kerala government, under Chief Minister Pattom Thanu Pillai, also supported a Trivandrum site.
⛪

Land was available

A church and a village said "yes".

The best spot held St. Mary Magdalene Church and a fishing village. The Bishop, Rev. Peter Bernard Pereira, and the people agreed to move. The church became the first office and lab. Today it is the ISRO Space Museum.
🌦️

Less rain

More good launch days.

Thumba gets less rain than the Kollam coast to the north. Fewer rainy days means more days fit for launches.
🛡️

Far from borders

Safe and quiet location.

At India's southern tip, Thumba is one of the places farthest from India's northern land borders.
🌐

Fills a gap on the globe

Why India needed its own station.

The electrojet is not the same everywhere. It is stronger over Peru than over India. To understand it, scientists needed rockets in the Asian sector too. That made Thumba useful to the whole world, and it was later dedicated to the United Nations.

Step 5

The story

From a fishing village to a space centre

1962
INCOSPAR (Indian National Committee for Space Research) is formed, led by Dr. Vikram Sarabhai. Its first job: set up TERLS, the Thumba Equatorial Rocket Launching Station.
21 Nov 1963
First launch: Nike-Apache, from beside St. Mary Magdalene Church. The Kerala Assembly adjourned so members could watch.
20 Nov 1967
First Indian-made rocket, RH-75, flies from Thumba.
2 Feb 1968
Prime Minister Indira Gandhi dedicates TERLS to the United Nations. Scientists from many countries use it.
1970–1993
Soviet M-100 weather rockets are launched from Thumba every week.
1971
After Sarabhai's death, the centre is renamed Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre (VSSC).
Today
Thumba still launches RH-series sounding rockets. Nearly 2,200 have flown so far. The old church is the ISRO Space Museum.

People behind it

  • Dr. Vikram Sarabhai: Father of the Indian space programme. Picked the site.
  • Dr. Homi Bhabha: Supported the project at the national level.
  • H.G.S. Murthy: First Director of TERLS.
  • Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam: Young engineer in the early Thumba team.
  • Bishop Peter Bernard Pereira and the people of Thumba: Gave their church and land for science.
